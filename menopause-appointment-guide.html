APPOINTMENT PREPARATION GUIDE

Preparing for a Menopause or Perimenopause Appointment

Practical steps to organise your symptoms, history and questions so you can make the most of your time with a healthcare professional.

Why preparation helps

Healthcare appointments are often short. Arriving with a clear, organised summary of your symptoms, menstrual history and current medications helps your doctor understand your experience and discuss suitable next steps. NICE guidance emphasises individualised care and shared decisions about managing menopause-associated symptoms.

This guide is for general information only. It does not replace medical advice, diagnosis or treatment. Always discuss your symptoms and any treatment decisions with a qualified healthcare professional.

1. Track your symptoms

Note which symptoms you are experiencing, how often they happen, how severe they are, and whether anything makes them better or worse. Commonly reported menopause-associated symptoms include:

  • vasomotor symptoms, such as hot flushes and night sweats;
  • sleep disturbance, including difficulty falling asleep or early waking;
  • mood changes, such as anxiety, low mood or irritability;
  • memory or concentration difficulties (sometimes described as "brain fog");
  • joint or muscle pain, headaches, or heart palpitations;
  • changes to your menstrual cycle, such as irregular, heavier or lighter bleeding;
  • genitourinary or sexual symptoms, such as vaginal dryness or low sexual desire.

Symptoms vary from person to person and can range from minor to severe, over short or long periods of time. Keeping a simple symptom diary for a few weeks before your appointment can give your doctor a clearer picture.

2. Note your menstrual history

Record the first day of your last period, your typical cycle length, and any recent changes (irregular, heavier, lighter, longer or shorter cycles). This information is clinically relevant: according to NICE guidance, menopause can be identified without laboratory tests in otherwise healthy people aged 45 or over who have not had a period for at least 12 months and are not using hormonal contraception.

Perimenopause may be identified when vasomotor symptoms have recently started alongside changes to the menstrual cycle. If you have had a hysterectomy, identification is based on the type and combination of symptoms rather than bleeding patterns.

3. List your medication and treatment history

Bring details of any medicines, supplements or treatments you are using, including:

  • hormone replacement therapy (HRT) or hormonal contraception;
  • any other prescribed or over-the-counter medicines;
  • previous treatments you have tried for menopause symptoms;
  • relevant diagnoses, surgeries or medical history.

Your doctor will consider the benefits and risks of any management options with you, tailored to your age, circumstances and medical history.

4. Questions to discuss with your doctor

You might want to ask:

  • Could my symptoms be related to menopause or perimenopause?
  • Are any tests needed, and what would they show?
  • What management options might suit me, and what are their benefits and risks?
  • If hormone replacement therapy is an option, what should I know about it?
  • Are there non-hormonal options that might help my symptoms?
  • When should I book a follow-up or review appointment?

How a structured briefing can help

MenoBrief organises your symptoms, menstrual history, medication details and the impact on daily life into a clear, doctor-friendly PDF briefing. Taking this summary to your appointment can support a focused conversation and help make sure nothing important is forgotten.

MenoBrief does not diagnose menopause, assign a menopause stage, or recommend treatment. It is an appointment preparation and communication tool only.
