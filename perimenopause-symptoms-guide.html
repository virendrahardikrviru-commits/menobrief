Understanding Perimenopause and Menopause Symptoms

What perimenopause and menopause are, common symptom categories, how to track symptoms, and why symptoms can have other causes.

What are perimenopause and menopause?

Menopause and perimenopause are a natural part of life. They happen when your periods change and eventually stop due to lower hormone levels. They usually affect women between 45 and 55, but can happen earlier, and they affect anyone who has periods.

Perimenopause is the time around menopause. According to NICE guidance, it may be identified in otherwise healthy people aged 45 or over when vasomotor symptoms (such as hot flushes and sweats) have recently started alongside any changes to the menstrual cycle.

Menopause is identified when a person has not had a period for at least 12 months and is not using hormonal contraception. If a person has had a hysterectomy, menopause is identified based on the type and combination of symptoms rather than bleeding patterns.

Common symptom categories

Menopause-associated symptoms vary from person to person. They can range from minor to severe and may be experienced over short or long periods. Commonly reported categories include:

  • Vasomotor: hot flushes, night sweats, feeling overheated.
  • Sleep: difficulty falling asleep, waking early, poor sleep quality.
  • Mood and emotional: anxiety, feeling on edge, mood swings, irritability, crying spells.
  • Cognitive: brain fog, memory issues, difficulty concentrating.
  • Physical: joint or muscle pain, headaches or migraines, heart palpitations, bloating or weight changes.
  • Menstrual: irregular periods, heavier or lighter bleeding, longer or shorter cycles.
  • Genitourinary and sexual: vaginal dryness, low sexual desire.

Not everyone experiences every symptom, and some people have few or no symptoms. There is no universal menopausal syndrome across all groups.

Symptom tracking

Keeping a simple record of your symptoms can help you and your doctor spot patterns and have a more productive conversation. You might note:

  • which symptoms you experience and when they started;
  • how often they happen and how severe they are;
  • possible triggers (for example, heat, stress, caffeine or alcohol);
  • how symptoms affect your work, sleep, relationships or daily activities;
  • changes to your periods and the date of your last period.

A few weeks of notes is often enough to give your healthcare professional a clearer picture of your experience.

Symptoms can have other causes

Symptoms such as tiredness, low mood, poor sleep, joint pain or changes to your periods can be associated with menopause, but they can also be caused by other health conditions. Symptoms should not automatically be attributed to menopause without discussion with a healthcare professional.

A healthcare professional can help determine whether your symptoms are related to menopause or to something else, and can advise on any tests or assessments that may be appropriate. For otherwise healthy people aged 45 or over, NICE guidance notes that perimenopause and menopause can generally be identified without laboratory tests.

When to speak to a healthcare professional

Consider speaking to a doctor or nurse if:

  • your symptoms are affecting your quality of life, work or relationships;
  • your periods become very heavy, very frequent or irregular;
  • you experience bleeding after your periods have stopped;
  • you are under 40 and think you may be experiencing menopause;
  • you are unsure what is causing your symptoms.
